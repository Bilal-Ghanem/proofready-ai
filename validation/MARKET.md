# ProofReady AI — market validation

**Evidence cut-off:** 4 September 2026
**Decision:** **Conditional GO for a 30-day concierge validation; NO-GO for SaaS build-out or paid acquisition today.**
**Product assessed:** a local-browser workflow that turns an organisation's actual AI uses, provider/deployer role, user context, literacy actions, evidence references, controls and review dates into an internal working file. It is not training, certification, legal advice or a compliance determination.

## Evidence discipline

- **Observed** means directly supported by the official datasets, regulator pages or vendor pages cited below and checked on 4 September 2026.
- **Calculated** means arithmetic using observed inputs; it is not measured demand.
- **Assumption** means a testable commercial hypothesis. No interviews, pre-orders, transactions, acquisition-cost data or retention data exist yet.
- Vendor claims describe their own products and are not independently validated. “No public price” means no usable amount was visible on the checked public page, not that the product is expensive.

## 1. Article 4: exact scope, timeline and enforcement nuance

### Current obligation

The original Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on **1 August 2024**, and Article 4 began applying on **2 February 2025**. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the AI Omnibus) entered into force on **27 July 2026** and amended Article 4. The Commission's current Q&A states that:

1. **Providers and deployers of AI systems** must take measures to support the development of AI literacy among staff and other persons dealing with operation and use of AI systems on their behalf.
2. They must take account of those people's **technical knowledge, experience, education and training**, plus the **context in which the systems are used**.
3. The 2026 amendment retained the organisational obligation but removed the requirement to guarantee a particular or “sufficient” level for an individual.
4. The Commission and Member States must support implementation, particularly by SMEs; the AI Board is to adopt recommendations and common objectives.

**Observed interpretation, not a new legal requirement:** the Commission suggests understanding AI and the systems actually used, identifying the organisation's role, considering system risk, and then tailoring literacy activity to people and context. It says there is no mandatory training format, certificate, AI officer or governance board. An organisation may keep internal records of training and/or other guidance. Simply copying one of the Commission's 40+ examples does not create a presumption of compliance. The Commission specifically says ordinary employee use of ChatGPT for advertising copy or translation is in scope and users should understand risks such as hallucination. [Commission AI Literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers), accessed 4 Sep 2026; [practice repository](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/repository-ai-literacy-practices), accessed 4 Sep 2026.

### Legal roles and territorial reach

“Provider” and “deployer” are legal roles that should be classified **per system**; they are not always synonyms for developer and customer. “Other persons” may include contractors, service providers and, depending on facts and risk, clients under the organisation's remit.

Article 2 makes the Act relevant to, among others: providers placing or putting AI systems into service in the EU regardless of where the provider is established; EU-established or EU-located deployers; and third-country providers or deployers where system output is used in the EU. It excludes, among other cases, purely personal non-professional deployment, certain military/defence/national-security uses, specific scientific R&D, and pre-market research/testing; open-source exclusions are qualified and do not cover every regulated situation. [Official AI Act Service Desk, Article 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2), accessed 4 Sep 2026.

Therefore “EU-facing” is only a prospecting heuristic. Whether an organisation, person or software feature is in scope is fact-specific and may need qualified legal advice.

### Timeline and enforcement

| Date | Observed position | Commercial implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Aug 2024 | AI Act entered into force | Historical, not a sales deadline |
| 2 Feb 2025 | Article 4 obligation began applying | The duty already applies; no countdown |
| 27 Jul 2026 | AI Omnibus entered into force and amended Article 4 | Claims must reflect the softer individual-level formulation |
| 2/3 Aug 2026 | National supervision/enforcement framework became active | Say “national supervision is now active,” not one universal deadline |

The Commission's pages contain a one-day inconsistency: its Article 4 Q&A says general supervision/enforcement rules apply from **3 August 2026**, then says national market-surveillance authorities start on **2 August 2026**; its broader AI Act timeline says authorities enforce from 2 August. National market-surveillance authorities—not the AI Office—supervise Article 4. Enforcement decisions and applicable national penalty rules can differ by Member State.

The Commission says authorities can impose penalties and other measures under national laws, proportionately and case by case, considering gravity and negligent/intentional character; an incident linked to inadequate guidance may make action more likely. The AI Act itself does not create a criminal offence or general right to compensation. **Do not market generic maximum AI Act fines as an automatic consequence of an Article 4 record gap.** [Commission AI Literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers) and [AI Act framework/timeline](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), accessed 4 Sep 2026.

### Product boundary

ProofReady can defensibly organise evidence; it cannot determine scope or compliance. The useful record is: AI use/system, organisational role, people affected, existing knowledge/experience, use context, relevant risk, selected literacy action, status/date, evidence reference, owner, review date and source/template version. Any score must be labelled an **operational completeness indicator**, never a legal readiness score.

For high-risk AI systems, separate duties—including trained human oversight and wider risk/documentation obligations—apply. A narrow Article 4 file is not a high-risk compliance programme.

## 2. Buyers and trigger events

| Priority | Economic buyer / working user | Job to be done | Trigger events | Disqualifiers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Owner/operations lead at a 10–100-person AI, software or digital agency | Join scattered tool use, roles, guidance and evidence into one client-reviewable internal file | client questionnaire; tender; new AI service; insurer/vendor review; AI policy refresh; staff rollout | no repeated AI use; only wants a generic certificate; mature GRC/LMS already joins the records |
| 2 | Solo/small AI-governance, privacy, DPO, HR or compliance consultancy | Reuse a structured workflow across client engagements while adding professional judgement | first Article 4 engagement; productising advisory work; client audit; recurring review | needs legal sign-off, cloud multi-tenancy or white-labelling immediately; prefers own spreadsheet |
| 3 | Risk/HR/IT owner in a 20–249-person professional-services firm | Establish ownership and evidence across distributed knowledge workers and contractors | board/risk review; procurement demand; new copilot rollout; staff turnover; incident | no named owner/budget; existing LMS/GRC already adequate |
| 4 | Non-EU agency serving EU customers | Answer contractual evidence requests and organise EU-use context | EU tender or customer due-diligence request | no EU output/use; territorial position too uncertain for self-service |

**Assumption:** consultant enablement may be the strongest wedge because one buyer has repeated client workflows and can supply the judgement the product lacks. This is not yet observed.

## 3. Bottom-up TAM, SAM and SOM

This sizing uses enterprise counts and adoption rates rather than a purchased market forecast. It measures an arithmetical pool at the proposed **€59 one-time Evidence Pack price**, not willingness to pay.

### TAM: EU AI-using enterprises with 10–249 workers

Eurostat's latest structural count available here is 2023 (dataset refreshed 1 Sep 2026). Its 2025 ICT survey reports AI adoption by employee band. Applying those rates gives:

| Employee band | EU enterprises | 2025 AI-use rate | Calculated AI-using enterprises |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10–19 | 1,023,841 | 17.00% proxy for 10–49 | 174,053 |
| 20–49 | 547,973 | 17.00% proxy for 10–49 | 93,155 |
| 50–249 | 248,817 | 30.36% | 75,547 |
| **Total** | **1,820,631** | — | **342,755** |

Calculation: `(1,023,841 + 547,973) × 0.17 + 248,817 × 0.3036 = 342,755` organisations.
**Revenue TAM:** `342,755 × €59 = €20,222,545` one-time.

This excludes micro-enterprises, financial services omitted from this ICT survey scope, public bodies and non-EU organisations, but simultaneously overstates the commercial market by assuming every measured AI user needs and buys a dedicated pack.

### SAM: professional/scientific/technical beachhead

For NACE M, Eurostat records 81,346 enterprises with 10–19 workers, 39,160 with 20–49, and 15,236 with 50–249. Applying the same size-band AI rates:

`(81,346 + 39,160) × 0.17 + 15,236 × 0.3036 = 25,115` AI-using organisations (rounded).
**Upper arithmetical SAM:** `25,115 × €59 = €1,481,785` one-time.

NACE M is much wider than agencies and consultancies, while some digital agencies sit elsewhere. A prudent planning range is **10,000–25,000 organisations**, or **€590,000–€1.48m** at €59. This is a proxy, not observed purchase intent.

### SOM: founder-constrained first year

A credible first-year obtainable range is **30–100 paid packs/setups**, determined by validation and founder-led distribution, not market share.

- Pack-only revenue equivalent: `30–100 × €59 = €1,770–€5,900`.
- Share of upper SAM organisations: `30/25,115 = 0.12%`; `100/25,115 = 0.40%`.
- If guided setups at €249 form part of the mix, revenue rises but delivery capacity falls; no mix is included in market size because no conversion evidence exists.

**Key sensitivity:** if only 10% of the proxy SAM has a distinct evidence-coordination problem and only 10% of those would pay, the theoretical pool falls to about `25,115 × 10% × 10% = 251` buyers, or only **€14,809** at €59. Price, incidence and willingness to pay are all assumptions.

## 4. Demand and pain evidence

### Observed evidence

- **Adoption is real and rising:** 20.0% of EU enterprises with 10+ workers used at least one measured AI technology in 2025, up from 13.5% in 2024. The 2025 rates were 17.0% for 10–49 and 30.36% for 50–249 enterprises. [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251211-2), published 11 Dec 2025.
- **Capability/legal uncertainty exists:** in `isoc_eb_ai`, 7.76% of all surveyed enterprises cited lack of relevant expertise as a reason not to use AI and 5.92% cited lack of clarity about legal consequences. These are adoption barriers, not ProofReady purchase intent.
- **Implementation is heterogeneous:** the Commission repository contains 40+ practices spanning e-learning, in-person training, bootcamps and industry/academia collaboration. Its explicit no-presumption warning supports the need for context but weakens any template-as-compliance pitch. [Commission repository](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/repository-ai-literacy-practices), accessed 4 Sep 2026.
- **Practical uncertainty is visible:** the Commission Q&A directly addresses certificates, records, contractors, ChatGPT, training format, governance officers and penalties. This is qualitative evidence of implementation questions, not quantified budget.

### Directional, weak signals

Checked 4 Sep 2026, Hacker News Algolia returned 186 stories matching “EU AI Act,” 70 matching “AI literacy,” and 27 matching “Article 4 AI Act” since 1 Jan 2025. GitHub issue search returned 17,197 matches for exact phrase “EU AI Act” created since that date. These broad text indexes contain false positives, bots and developer discussion; they are **not search volume or buyer intent**. [HN Algolia API](https://hn.algolia.com/api); [GitHub Search API](https://api.github.com/search/issues?q=%22EU%20AI%20Act%22%20created:%3E=2025-01-01).

A January 2026 Show HN for open-source, offline-first EuConform received 71 points and 49 comments, signalling developer interest while simultaneously demonstrating free/local competition. [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46557823), accessed 4 Sep 2026.

### Missing proof

There is no reliable free keyword-volume evidence, customer interview data, pre-order, paid transaction, landing conversion, CAC or renewal signal. Regulation and category activity validate a **problem class**, not ProofReady's workflow, price or route to market.

## 6. Positioning and acquisition

Defensible positioning:

> Build a reviewable AI-literacy working file from your real tools, roles and learning actions. Local to your browser; no generic certificate and no compliance guarantee.

Avoid “Article 4 compliant,” “audit-proof,” “certified,” “avoid fines,” a legal “readiness score,” mandatory-course claims and deadline countdowns.

### Channels ranked by learning value

1. **Founder-led, trigger-based interviews with consultants and 10–100-person AI/digital agencies.** Lowest-cost route to test the job, wording and price. Measure independently stated pain, not polite interest.
2. **Consultant/DPO/HR partner pilot.** Potential for repeated client use and guided revenue. Test a reusable separate-client workflow before reseller or multi-tenant development.
3. **High-intent utility pages/checklists.** Examples: “AI literacy evidence checklist,” “Article 4 records for agencies,” and “AI-use inventory linked to literacy actions.” Measure starts, qualified leads and paid conversion; do not assume SEO volume.
4. **Practitioner communities/webinars.** Useful regulator clarification can build trust, but channel rules and external-post approval are prerequisites.
5. **Paid search only after unpaid conversion proof.** A €59 price creates little CAC headroom; paid traffic is more plausible for guided setups or bundles.

Trigger lists should prioritise visible tenders, AI service launches, copilot rollouts, client questionnaires, governance hiring, policy refreshes and insurer/procurement reviews—not generic “you use AI” messaging.

## 7. Why this may fail

1. **The manual substitute wins:** no prescribed certificate or format means a spreadsheet, template and LMS may be enough.
2. **Reduced urgency:** the 2026 amendment softened the individual-level formulation; fear-led demand assumptions are inaccurate.
3. **Wrong job:** buyers may primarily need quality training, legal interpretation, high-risk controls or technical monitoring, none of which ProofReady provides.
4. **Episodic use:** initial setup may not recur often enough for subscription retention.
5. **Trust deficit:** a new brand lacks regulator, legal and customer authority. Local-first reduces upload concerns but not credibility risk.
6. **Consultants resist:** they may prefer proprietary sheets or see software as commoditising billable judgement.
7. **Incumbents move down-market:** trail already combines registry and literacy; broad suites can launch starter offers.
8. **Self-service errors:** users may misclassify systems/roles or mistake completeness for compliance.
9. **Price mismatch:** €59 can signal “template toy,” while €249 guided delivery may underprice founder time and liability-sensitive support.
10. **No moat:** a static local app is copyable. Defensibility would require trusted workflow, maintained sources, partner distribution and learned implementation patterns.
11. **Market-size illusion:** 342,755 AI users does not mean 342,755 buyers; the actual paid niche could be hundreds.
12. **Enforcement does not create budget:** proportionate national enforcement and no mandated record format can produce awareness without purchase urgency.

## 8. Objective go/no-go criteria

**GO only for a 30-day, no-spend concierge test** using the existing local MVP. Before accounts, cloud sync, broad governance features or paid acquisition, require all of:

- **10 qualified conversations**, including at least 5 agencies and 3 multi-client consultants.
- At least **3/10 independently describe** the inventory → roles/context → literacy action/evidence coordination problem before seeing the product.
- At least **3 written paid-pilot commitments at ≥€59**, including either one **€249 guided setup** or one consultant expecting reuse across ≥3 clients.
- At least **4/10 say the joined workflow is materially better** than their current spreadsheet/LMS process and explain why.
- At least **2 actual payments** after terms/payment/privacy are separately approved; written intent alone is not enough for build-out.
- Fewer than **2/10 misunderstand** the output as certification/compliance after seeing the disclaimer; repeated misunderstanding requires product/copy redesign.
- At least **2 users return for a review/update** within 30 days or consultants identify a concrete multi-client repeat loop before any subscription test.

**NO-GO / stop** if fewer than 2 paid commitments emerge from 10 qualified calls, or if buyers consistently value only free templates/certificates. **Pivot to training partnership/referral** if training dominates. **Pivot to consultant client packs** if consultants show clearly stronger pull. **No public SaaS build today** because willingness to pay, acquisition cost and repeat use remain unmeasured.
